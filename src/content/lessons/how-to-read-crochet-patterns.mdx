A crochet pattern is a set of small instructions. Once you know the shorthand, a line like **(sc, inc) × 6 (18)** becomes a simple counting exercise rather than a puzzle.

**Thread & Petal lessons use US crochet terms** unless a pattern explicitly says otherwise. UK names can refer to a *different stitch* even when the words look familiar.

## US and UK stitch names

| Stitch you make | US name / abbreviation | UK name / abbreviation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A chain | Chain, **ch** | Chain, **ch** |
| Joining stitch | Slip stitch, **sl st** | Slip stitch, **ss** or **sl st** |
| Short basic stitch | Single crochet, **sc** | Double crochet, **dc** |
| Medium-height stitch | Half double crochet, **hdc** | Half treble, **htr** |
| Taller stitch | Double crochet, **dc** | Treble, **tr** |
| Very tall stitch | Treble crochet, **tr** | Double treble, **dtr** |

**Watch out:** a US *dc* and a UK *dc* are **not the same stitch**. Use the terminology indicated by the pattern's author, not your own country, to interpret it.

## The abbreviations you'll see most often

- **ch**: chain.
- **sc**, **hdc**, **dc**: US single, half double, and double crochet.
- **sl st**: slip stitch.
- **st / sts**: stitch / stitches.
- **sp**: space; **ch-sp**: a chain-created space.
- **yo**: yarn over.
- **inc**: increase; **dec**: decrease (read the pattern notes for the exact method).
- **sc2tog**: work two single crochets together to make one stitch.
- **BLO / FLO**: back-loop-only / front-loop-only.
- **rnd**: round; **rep**: repeat.
- **RS / WS**: right side / wrong side.

Some designers invent abbreviations for special stitches. Always read the **stitch key** near the beginning.

## Decode a repeat

Suppose the pattern says:

**Round 3: (1 sc, inc) × 6. (18 sts)**

Read it in this order:

1. Make **one single crochet** in the next stitch.
2. Work **two single crochets** into the following stitch (one increase).
3. Repeat those two actions **six times total**.
4. The round should finish with **18 stitches**.

Each repeat uses two old stitches and makes three new stitches. Six repeats use 12 stitches from the preceding round and produce 18 stitches.

## What do asterisks and brackets mean?

Patterns may write repeats as `*sc, inc; repeat from * around`, `(sc, inc) x 6`, or `[sc, inc] six times`. The intent is often similar, but different pattern publishers use punctuation differently. **Follow the legend in the actual pattern** if you're uncertain.

Numbers at the end of a row or round, such as **(24)**, usually indicate the **stitch count** at that point rather than another instruction.

## Joined rounds or spiral rounds?

A pattern for a flat motif may say **join with sl st** and begin the next round with chains. A stuffed amigurumi pattern often says to **work in a continuous spiral** and use a marker. The exact joining rule changes where the next stitch is placed.

If you're not sure which method to follow, stop at the end of the round and check the introductory notes.

## Learn to read your materials list

- **5 mm** is a hook diameter, not a yarn thickness.
- **50 g / 1.76 oz** describes yarn mass; **yards / meters** describe length.
- **Gauge** (often called *tension* in UK patterns) is the number of stitches and rows in a measured swatch.
- Yarn categories and hook letters can vary across countries; use **millimeters** as your reliable hook-size reference.

## Try translating one real line

Read: **Ch 1, turn, sc in each of next 10 sts. (10)**

You make a chain, turn the work, then single crochet into each of the next ten stitches, finishing with ten stitches. Whether that turning chain counts is determined by the specific pattern's instructions.

## A reliable reference

These abbreviations and the US/UK comparison follow the [Craft Yarn Council's standard crochet abbreviations](https://craftyarncouncil.com/node/31). The Council also publishes a [standard stitch-symbol guide](https://craftyarncouncil.com/standards/crochet-chart-symbols) for reading diagrams. Individual patterns still take precedence when defining special stitches.
