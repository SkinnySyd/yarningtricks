Most crocheters undo stitches. It's part of making things, not evidence that you're doing it wrong. The useful skill is noticing *where* the mistake happened and choosing whether to correct it.

## Your stitch count is wrong

If the pattern says the row should have 10 stitches but you count 9 or 11:

1. Lay your work down so the upper V shapes are easy to see.
2. Count complete stitches, leaving out the loop currently on your hook.
3. Look closely at the **first and last stitches** of the row.
4. Check whether the turning chain counts as a stitch in **this particular pattern**.
5. Undo back to the mistake if needed and work forward again.

An unexplained stitch-count change is easier to fix immediately than after another ten rows.

## How to undo crochet without making a mess

Crocheters often call unraveling **frogging**.

1. Remove your hook from the working loop while holding that loop securely.
2. Gently pull the working yarn until stitches undo to the point you want to correct.
3. Put your hook back into the **active loop**.
4. Check that the loop is oriented correctly and the yarn isn't split before continuing.

If you're pausing mid-project, thread a removable marker through the active loop so it can't pull out accidentally.

## When your edges zigzag

Missing or extra edge stitches are common. Try a contrasting marker in your first and last stitch; it helps you see where the next row begins and ends. See our [straight-edges lesson](/learn/crochet-straight-edges/) for a ten-stitch practice exercise.

## When stitches are too tight or too loose

- **Too tight:** use a comfortable grip, let the yarn feed more freely, or experiment with a larger hook.
- **Too loose:** check whether the hook size is much larger than the yarn label recommends; practice pulling up loops to a consistent height.
- **Uneven tension:** work a small swatch. Consistency usually improves with repetition rather than forcing the yarn into shape.

If you're making amigurumi, holes can show stuffing. A smaller hook relative to the yarn often helps create denser fabric, but avoid crocheting so tightly that your hands strain.

## Fasten off cleanly

At the end of a normal crochet piece:

1. Cut the working yarn, leaving a useful tail—roughly **10–15 cm (4–6 in)** is common for weaving in, though projects may need longer tails for sewing.
2. Pull the cut tail through your remaining loop, and gently tighten to secure it.
3. Thread the tail onto a tapestry needle.
4. Weave it through several nearby stitches on the **wrong side**, changing direction when possible so the end is more secure.
5. Trim the last bit only after you've checked that the tail is secure and the project lies flat.

Different fibers and uses may require different finishing methods. A decorative item that won't be washed may need less reinforcement than a toy or blanket.

## Join two crocheted pieces

For a simple sewn join:

1. Position the pieces and hold them in place using removable markers.
2. Thread a yarn needle with a long matching yarn tail.
3. Sew through corresponding stitches along the edges without pulling so tightly that the fabric puckers.
4. Check the position from the front before tying off and weaving in the remaining tail.

For stuffed characters, pin or mark ears and limbs before sewing so they're balanced. If the item is intended for a very young child, avoid detachable small parts or loose embellishments and check appropriate toy-safety requirements.

## Block your work when it helps

**Blocking** means gently shaping a crocheted piece using moisture or steam suitable for its fiber. Check the yarn care instructions before trying it. Simple cotton motifs can often be shaped lightly; some synthetic fibers can be damaged by high heat.

Blocking can straighten a motif's edges, but it won't fix an incorrect stitch count or replace a missing row.

## Your finishing checklist

Before calling the project done, check: **stitch count, securely woven ends, even joins, correct size, and any care guidance** for your yarn.
