A **magic ring** (also called a **magic circle**) lets you begin crocheting around an adjustable loop. You can tighten its center after working the first stitches—handy for amigurumi heads, small circles, and flowers.

For a first attempt, use smooth medium-weight yarn and a hook that lets you see each loop.

## Make a magic ring with six single crochets

There are several valid ways to form the loop. Here's one beginner-friendly approach:

1. Lay the yarn across your fingers, leaving a tail around **15–20 cm (6–8 in)** long.
2. Wrap the yarn into a circle so the working yarn crosses over the tail. Hold the crossing point with your fingers. The opening is your adjustable ring.
3. Insert the crochet hook **through the center of the ring**, catch the working yarn, and pull up a loop.
4. Yarn over and chain **1** to secure the working loop. In this exercise, that chain **does not count** as a stitch.
5. Insert the hook through the ring again, under the circle's strands; yarn over and pull up a loop. You have two loops on the hook.
6. Yarn over and pull through both loops: that's **one single crochet**.
7. Repeat until you have **6 sc worked into the ring**. Work over the yarn tail where practical so it remains accessible for tightening.
8. Carefully pull the yarn **tail** to draw the opening closed. Count your six V-shaped stitches around the outside.

A magic ring relies on a secure tail. For items that will be handled or washed, weave the tail back through nearby stitches in more than one direction to help prevent the center loosening.

## No magic ring? Use a chain ring

For a larger, less adjustable opening, **chain 4, slip stitch into the first chain to form a ring**, and work your first stitches into the middle. This is a useful alternative for granny squares and decorative motifs. The center will not tighten in quite the same way.

## Continuous spiral rounds

In spiral crochet, you **do not join** the end of one round to its beginning. The next stitch flows straight into the first stitch of the next round.

1. After your six initial sc, place a marker in the **first stitch of the next round**.
2. Work the next round as instructed (for example, **2 sc in each stitch** for an increase round, producing 12 stitches).
3. Move the marker each time you return to the beginning of a new round.

Spirals are common in amigurumi because they avoid a visible joining seam.

## Joined rounds

In a **joined round**, you finish a round with a slip stitch into the designated starting stitch, then begin the next round as your pattern instructs. Joined rounds may use a starting chain; whether it counts as a stitch varies by design.

**Do not mix the two methods casually.** If a pattern is written for joined rounds, following it as a spiral can shift stitch placement and alter the shape.

## Count and mark your rounds

Keep the stitch marker in the first stitch of each spiral round, not on your hook. At the end of the round, count the stitches and move the marker before starting the next. The working loop itself is not counted.

## Troubleshooting a loose center

If the ring stays open, check whether you're pulling the tail rather than the working yarn. If the yarn splits or catches, loosen your hold and adjust the loops gently instead of forcing them.
