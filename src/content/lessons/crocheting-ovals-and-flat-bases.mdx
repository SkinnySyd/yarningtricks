A circle begins at a center point. An **oval** often begins with a short chain: you crochet along one side of it, turn around the end, and work back along the other side. The two rounded ends make the shape longer without turning it into a rectangle.

We'll make a small, flat oval with clear stitch totals. Use US **single crochet (sc)** and **increase (inc = 2 sc in one stitch)** throughout.

## Make a 16-stitch practice oval

With smooth yarn and a hook you can use comfortably:

1. **Chain 6.** There are five available chain positions after the one nearest your hook is skipped.
2. **Round 1:** Work 2 sc in the **second chain from the hook**. Work 1 sc in each of the next **3 chains**. Work **4 sc in the last chain**. Rotate the foundation so you can work along the opposite underside. Make 1 sc in each of the next **3 unused underside loops**, then **2 sc in the underside of the first worked chain**. **[14 sc total]**
3. Place a stitch marker into the first stitch of the next round. **Round 2:** Work 2 sc in each of the next **2 stitches**, then 1 sc in each of the next **3**; 2 sc in each of the next **4** end stitches; 1 sc in each of the next **3** side stitches; and 2 sc in each of the final **2** end stitches. **[22 sc]**

**Count check:** round 1 is 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = **14**. Round 2 turns the eight end stitches into sixteen, plus six straight-side stitches = **22**.

Each chain position can be entered from opposite sides of the foundation; don't invent extra stitches between them. The work must turn around the ends of the chain, **not turn as if starting a new flat row**.

## What if it curls?

A gentle curl can happen because a tight foundation chain restricts the center, while the outside edge is growing. If the work is becoming a little bowl instead of a flat base, inspect where you put the end increases and whether your foundation is too tight.

If the edge **ruffles**, there may be too many increases for your yarn and tension. If the edge **cups**, you may have too few increases or a tight central chain. Different yarns and oval proportions need different increase distributions; these two rounds are a controlled exercise, not a universal rule for every oval.

## How flat bases become three-dimensional

Once a base reaches the width required by a pattern, subsequent rounds may use **no increases**. The sides then tend to rise, turning a flat oval into the bottom of a bag, a foot, or a shallow basket.

Some patterns create a sharper corner by working a round in [back loops only](/learn/front-back-loops-surface-crochet/). That leaves a ridge and can help define the turn. The orientation of the piece matters, so don't add a BLO round unless it fits the intended shape.

## Troubleshooting under the hook

| Symptom | Likely first check |
| --- | --- |
| Oval twists into a corkscrew | Was the foundation chain very tight? |
| One end looks narrower | Were the end increases distributed symmetrically? |
| Stitch count is off | Did you crochet into the underside of the foundation and count every end stitch? |
| Base waves around the edges | Are you increasing too quickly for the yarn and shape? |
| The piece looks like a circle | Try a longer starting chain for a more elongated oval |

For a closer look at avoiding missed stitches, read [crocheting an oval without losing your place](/blog/crochet-an-oval-without-losing-your-place/) and revisit [increases and decreases](/learn/crochet-increases-and-decreases/).

## One useful experiment

Repeat round 1 in a second color. Put markers at the two rounded ends and label the straight sides. Understanding **where** the shaping happens matters more than memorizing a list of numbers.

When that makes sense, you're ready to recognize flat bases in [free crochet patterns](/patterns/).
