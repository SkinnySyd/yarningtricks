If your crochet rectangle slowly turns into a triangle, you're probably not doing anything mysterious—you're likely adding or skipping a stitch at the edge. This lesson makes those edges easier to see.

## Know what a row looks like

A **row** runs across a piece of fabric. After finishing one row, most flat crochet patterns ask you to **turn your work** so you can crochet back in the other direction. A turning chain brings the hook up to approximately the height of the stitch you're making.

Whether that chain **counts as a stitch** is decided by your pattern, not by a universal rule.

## Find the first and last stitches

1. Work a practice row of ten single crochets.
2. Mark the **first actual stitch** with a removable marker or a small strand of contrasting yarn.
3. Mark the **last actual stitch** with a second marker.
4. Look along the upper edge. Each completed sc has a V made of two loops.
5. Count ten Vs before starting your next row.

**Try this:** chain 1, turn, and single crochet into the first stitch. Work across all ten stitches. Count ten again. In this exercise the chain-1 **does not count**.

## What exactly does "turn" mean?

When you reach the end of a flat row, rotate your work as you would turn a page. The side of the fabric that faced away from you will now face you. Keep the working yarn attached and follow the stated turning-chain instruction.

**Do not assume** the chain belongs to the first or last stitch count. Read the first row of the pattern carefully; it should establish the convention.

## How to count rows

For single crochet, you can often see stacked horizontal ridges or side "bumps" along the fabric. For taller stitches, watch the rows of vertical posts. To count accurately, lay the fabric flat and identify a complete row from one edge to the other; use a row counter if you find your place easily slips.

When following a pattern with repeats, adding a small removable marker every fifth or tenth row can save time.

## Why is my piece getting smaller?

- You may be missing the first stitch, especially if it's tucked close to the turning chain.
- You may be stopping one stitch before the real last stitch.
- Your tension may be tightening, making the fabric *look* narrower even when the count is correct.

Count stitches to distinguish the first two problems from tension.

## Why is my piece getting wider?

You may be working an extra stitch into a turning chain that the pattern says **does not count** as a stitch, or working twice into the edge stitch.

If the count is 11 instead of 10, find the accidental increase and either undo the row or follow a deliberate correction that keeps the fabric even.

## A quick edge-check habit

After each row, pause and ask: **same number of stitches? correct turning chain? did I work the final stitch?** This 10-second check prevents a lot of unraveling later.
