A neat row is not made by pulling the yarn as tightly as possible. It's made by giving each loop enough room to form comfortably, over and over. Let's find a rhythm that suits *your* hands.

## What tension really means

**Tension** is how firmly you hold and feed yarn as you crochet. Your hook size, yarn texture, and the way you draw up each loop also affect stitch size. No single grip is correct for every maker.

A little variation is normal. What matters is that your fabric behaves as the project needs: relaxed for a soft scarf, or closely stitched for a stuffed crochet animal.

## A five-minute tension experiment

You'll need smooth, light-colored yarn, a suitable hook, and a [stitch marker](/learn/crochet-supplies-for-beginners/) if you find counting tricky.

1. Make a slip knot and **chain 16**. Work 15 single crochets into the chains, starting in the second chain from your hook. **[15 sc]**
2. Chain 1 and turn. For this exercise, the turning chain **does not count** as a stitch. Make 15 sc across.
3. Repeat the second row four more times. Count **15 sc** at the end of each row.
4. Lay the swatch flat, without stretching. Compare the first row with the last. Are the stitches getting shorter or the edges narrower, even though the counts match?
5. Work another swatch, this time consciously allowing the working yarn to slide through your guiding fingers.

If the second swatch is more comfortable and more even, you've found a useful adjustment. If both look good, that's good information too.

## Find a relaxed yarn hold

Try draping the yarn over a guiding finger, then letting it pass loosely through the other fingers. You can wrap it once if that helps regulate the flow, but avoid winding it tightly around your hand. Keep your shoulders relaxed and periodically release your grip.

When you make a single crochet, pull the loop up to roughly the height of the stitch you're working into. A loop drawn much higher can become loose; a loop pulled down hard can make the next stitch difficult to enter. Compare a few stitches before changing your technique again.

**Comfort check:** fingers going numb, sharp pain, or persistent aching are reasons to stop and rest, not to force another round.

## What's the difference between tension and gauge?

**Tension** describes how you work. **Gauge** is the number of stitches and rows that fit into a measured area of finished fabric. Gauge is the observable result of your tension, yarn, hook, and stitch pattern. Two crocheters can make the same stitch count and still end up with pieces of different sizes.

For amigurumi, focus first on **dense but workable fabric**. Make a small sample and gently stretch it as stuffing might. If noticeable holes appear, try a slightly smaller hook or a better-suited yarn. If the hook keeps snagging and your hands strain, try a slightly larger hook. See [choosing yarn for amigurumi](/blog/choosing-yarn-for-amigurumi/) for a fuller materials check.

## Troubleshooting at the making table

| What you notice | First thing to check | Small adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook struggles to enter stitches | Loops are very tight | Relax the yarn hold or try a slightly larger hook |
| Fabric has gaps | Stitches too open for stuffing | Test a smaller hook or a different yarn |
| Edges shrink but stitch count is correct | Tension changing along rows | Compare loop height and take regular breaks |
| Stitches vary wildly | Yarn feeding unevenly | Let the ball unwind freely and keep a steady hold |
| Hands tire quickly | Grip and posture | Loosen grip, reposition your work, and rest |

If the stitch **count** changes, that's a different problem. The [straight-edge lesson](/learn/crochet-straight-edges/) shows how to catch skipped edge stitches before blaming tension.

## A little practice to keep

Make three 15-stitch rows after a short break, rather than a marathon swatch. Write down your yarn, hook size, and what felt comfortable. Revisit your notes when you begin [your first amigurumi friend](/blog/your-first-amigurumi-friend/).

The goal is not to make every loop identical under a magnifying glass. It's to enjoy a fabric you can make again tomorrow.
