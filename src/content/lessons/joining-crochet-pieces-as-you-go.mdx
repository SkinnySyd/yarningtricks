Sometimes two little pieces need to become one **before** the project is finished. Think of a pair of legs becoming a body, or two tiny toes forming a foot. A join-as-you-go method builds them into a shared round instead of sewing every part on afterward.

There are several ways to join crochet pieces. Here's one **practice-only** example that makes the stitch math visible; a particular animal pattern may use a different joining order.

## Make two small tabs

Use smooth yarn and US single crochet (sc).

1. **Tab A:** Chain 5, work 4 sc beginning in the second chain from your hook. Chain 1 and turn; make 4 sc across for one more row. Fasten off with a tail.
2. **Tab B:** Repeat the same two rows, but **do not fasten off**. You have two matching flat tabs, each 4 stitches wide.
3. Lay them side by side with their upper edges facing the same way. The working yarn is attached to Tab B. Place a marker at its first stitch.

We will join **their top edges into one flat row**, with a small chain bridge. This is not a round and will not form a closed tube.

## Bridge the gap, then count

1. With the working yarn on Tab B, work **4 sc across Tab B's top edge**. **[4]**
2. **Chain 2** to span the gap. These chains will each become one stitch on the next row.
3. Insert the hook into the first stitch on the top edge of Tab A and make **4 sc** across. **[4]**
4. You now have a connected line of **4 sc + 2 chains + 4 sc = 10 positions**.
5. Chain 1 and turn. Work **4 sc, 2 sc into the two bridge chains, and 4 sc** across. **[10 sc]**

This simple join shows why chains used to bridge a space count as real places to work into. At the join, avoid pulling the bridge tight enough to bunch the tabs together.

## How is joining in a round different?

For amigurumi legs or toes, a pattern may ask you to crochet around the first piece, chain between pieces, crochet around the second, and then return along the opposite side of the bridge. That forms a **continuous perimeter**, not the flat row above.

**Important counting rule:** if you make a bridge of two chains and later work along **both sides** of it, that bridge can contribute **four stitches** to the perimeter (two along each side), *if the pattern instructs you to work them*. Count the two joined pieces as well. Don't add the chain stitches twice when the instructions use only one side.

Always mark the joining point, follow the designer's stated direction, and verify the first shared round total before continuing.

## Keep the pieces facing the right way

Before joining, check that both pieces have their **right sides facing consistently** and that any details point outward. Pin them together temporarily to visualize the final orientation.

If one part twists when you connect it, undo the bridge and reorient the piece. Adding stuffing won't correct a twist in the fabric.

## A few joining problems, solved

**There's a hole between the pieces:** a loose bridge or a gap at its ends may be the cause. Check the chain length and tension before trying to close it with extra stitches.

**The new row has 11 stitches instead of 10:** look for an accidental increase where the bridge meets a tab.

**The join puckers:** the bridge may be too short, or you're pulling tightly while crossing the gap.

**Two toes face different directions:** check their orientation before the shared round; do not assume they'll straighten once stuffed.

## When to sew instead

Joining as you crochet can create a continuous construction, but it isn't automatically stronger or better than sewing. Use [sewing amigurumi pieces together](/learn/sew-amigurumi-pieces-together/) when you need flexible positioning or the pattern calls for individual attachments.
