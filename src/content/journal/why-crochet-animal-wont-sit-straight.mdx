You finish your crochet animal, set it down to admire it, and—plop—it leans sideways. Or its cheerful head pulls the whole body forward. A tiny wobble can be charming; a surprise topple can be frustrating.

Before undoing your hard work, it helps to ask **what part of the toy is moving the weight out of balance**.

Not every amigurumi design is meant to stand without support. Some are floppy, some sit, and some are designed to lean on a shelf. First compare your finished friend with the posture intended by its pattern.

## Start with the surface, not the stitches

Place the toy on a level, firm table. A soft blanket or sloping shelf can make a well-constructed toy look unstable.

Turn the toy gently to see where it tips. Does it roll toward the heavier head? Does one foot touch the table before the other? Do the legs splay more on one side?

These observations will help you choose a fix that fits the actual problem.

## Five little causes of a leaning friend

### 1. One foot is fuller than the other

Compare paired feet or paws with your fingers. A small difference in stuffing can change the way they rest. If an opening is still accessible, remove or redistribute the filling in small amounts, following the pattern.

### 2. The feet are attached at different heights

Use a removable yarn strand to mark the centerline. Count the body rounds and stitches to the attachment points, rather than relying only on how they look from the front.

If one foot or leg sits a round higher, reposition it before fastening off. See our [amigurumi assembly lesson](/learn/sew-amigurumi-pieces-together/) for a calm way to check matching pieces.

### 3. The head is pulling forward

A big crocheted head changes the balance of a small body. Check that the neck seam follows the pattern's placement and is evenly secured. A loose seam can let a head flop; an off-center seam can tilt it.

Adding a lot more stuffing isn't always the answer. It may stretch the fabric and make the head heavier without improving the join.

### 4. The body's base is too rounded

Some body shapes are intentionally soft spheres. Others have a slightly wider, flatter bottom or separate feet that create a support area.

Look at the shaping instructions. Did the last decrease rounds match their stitch counts? Was the underside pulled into a point? If the base is meant to be flat but has become lumpy, adjust the stuffing before sewing the opening closed.

### 5. An asymmetrical detail changed the balance

A large tail, raised wing or heavy decorative tuft can pull a lightweight toy off-center. Check whether the pattern intends that detail to be supported by another part of the body. Don't move pieces to new positions until you've compared the construction notes.

## A simple balance check before permanent sewing

1. Stuff the head and body as instructed.
2. Temporarily pin the limbs, tail and head in position.
3. Set the animal on a flat surface, supporting it gently as needed.
4. Look from the front, back and side.
5. Adjust an attachment **one stitch or round at a time**, only where the pattern allows.
6. Photograph the arrangement, then sew the parts once you're happy with it.

For a character that is supposed to sit, test its **sitting posture** rather than trying to make it stand. Not all designs need both.

## What not to try

**Don't force a smaller hook into a nearly finished animal** to make it stiffer. Hook size matters when crocheting the fabric, not after the toy is assembled.

**Don't pack the head with more and more filling** just to make it hold still. A firm, well-placed join usually matters more than excessive stuffing.

**Don't add loose weighted beads** to a toy intended for a young child. Small internal components can create risks if seams fail. Avoid ad-hoc weight fixes without an appropriate safety assessment.

## A little bit of wonk is allowed

Handmade toys have character. Your crocheted friend doesn't have to balance like a manufactured figurine to deserve a spot on the shelf.

But if a noticeable lean makes it difficult to enjoy your creation, begin with the **smallest reversible change**. Check the surface. Check matching feet. Check the attachment points. Then investigate the filling.
