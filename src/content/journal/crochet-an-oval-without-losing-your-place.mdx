You know that lovely moment when a crochet circle begins to curve into a little head? An **oval** is its close cousin. Instead of growing outward from one small center, the stitches travel around a short chain, making a longer, rounded shape.

Ovals turn up in all sorts of amigurumi places: small feet, muzzles, flat bases, and details that need a little more length than a circle can give them. They're useful once you understand the little journey your hook is taking.

## A circle begins in the middle. An oval begins with a line.

A round shape often starts with a [magic ring](/learn/magic-ring-crochet-in-the-round/). An oval usually begins with a **foundation chain**.

Imagine the chain as a tiny path. You crochet along one side, curve around the far end, and then work back along the *underside of the same chain*. At the other end, a few more stitches help the path turn smoothly.

You're not making two separate rows and joining them together. You're traveling around one starting chain.

If chains still feel mysterious, practice a few with our [first crochet chain tutorial](/patterns/first-crochet-chain/) before trying this.

## A little practice oval: 12 stitches

This is a **standalone practice exercise**, not a replacement for the starting rounds in any particular animal pattern. It uses US crochet terms.

You'll need a small amount of smooth yarn, a suitable hook, and a stitch marker. Try a light yarn color so you can see the two sides of the chain.

1. **Chain 6.** Keep the chains relaxed enough to find their loops. The loop on your hook does not count as a chain.
2. **Single crochet in the next 4 chains**, beginning in the second chain from the hook. You've made 4 single crochets.
3. **Work 3 single crochets in the last chain.** These stitches share one space and curve around the far end. You now have 7 stitches.
4. **Rotate the work, rather than turning it like a flat row.** Find the unused loops along the underside of your foundation chain.
5. **Single crochet into the underside of the next 3 chains.** You now have 10 stitches.
6. **Work 2 single crochets into the underside of the first chain you crocheted into.** This completes the near end of the oval.

Count your stitches: **4 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 12**. The first end already has one stitch on its upper side, so those last two stitches make three around that end altogether.

Place a marker in the first stitch of the oval if you plan to practice another round. Look for an elongated little ring, not a perfect circle.

> Your first oval may look slightly untidy. That's okay. For now, the goal is to understand where the hook goes and finish with the right count.

## Where do the increases go next?

On a flat oval, the longer sides tend to grow more slowly than the rounded ends. In later rounds, the increases are usually concentrated near those two curves, while the side stitches help preserve the length.

The placement and stitch counts depend on the design. A narrow sole, a wide snout, and a rounded base may use quite different increase plans. **Follow the pattern you're making**, rather than assuming every oval grows the same way.

The principle is simple: evenly spaced shaping at the ends helps the fabric lie flatter. Too few increases can pull the edges upward; too many can make the oval ripple.

## Three little mistakes to watch for

### Working back into the same top loops

After the first end, your hook needs to find the *unused underside* of the chain. If you accidentally crochet back through the same loops you already used, the shape may bunch or fold.

Turn the piece gently in your fingers and look for the remaining chain bumps.

### Losing the first stitch

The two ends look very similar once the shape grows. Mark the first stitch when the oval is still small, especially if the next round will be worked in a spiral.

A bit of contrasting yarn works just as well as a fancy marker.

### Forgetting to count the turning stitches

Several stitches worked into one chain still count as separate stitches. If you put three single crochets into the end chain, that's **three**, not one.

Check your count before making another round. Our [pattern-reading lesson](/learn/how-to-read-crochet-patterns/) can help with repeats and numbers in brackets.

## What if your oval keeps curling?

First, make sure the pattern is meant to stay flat. A little curve might be exactly what a stuffed muzzle or foot needs.

For a flat practice piece, check your stitch count and the number of increases around the ends. Then look at your tension. Very tight chains can make the middle pull inward, while loose stitches can leave an open-looking center.

Try again with a more relaxed foundation chain rather than forcing the fabric flat.

## A shape worth keeping in your stitch basket

Once you've tried an oval on its own, it will be easier to recognize one hiding inside a longer project. You might notice the same shape-making idea when looking at a [Meadow Giraffe](/patterns/meadow-giraffe-amigurumi/), a [Coastal Seahorse](/patterns/coastal-seahorse-amigurumi/), or another friend from the [amigurumi pattern basket](/patterns/?category=Amigurumi). Follow each project's actual instructions for its pieces.

A few stitches on one side, a gentle turn, a few stitches on the other. That's all the little oval wants to teach you today.
