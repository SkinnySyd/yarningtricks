You have followed every round, counted every stitch, and sewn on the ears. So why does your little animal look a bit different from the picture?

Because a crochet pattern describes **a process**, not a machine-made duplicate. Yarn, hook size, the height of your stitches, how you stuff, and where you place a face all affect the final character. Some differences are harmless variations; others are useful clues that something needs correcting.

## Start with the stitch count, not the photo

Compare each round total with the pattern. If the numbers match, the underlying construction may be correct even when the finished shape differs. If they don't match, review your increases and decreases *before* trying to fix the silhouette with stuffing.

Our [pattern-reading lesson](/learn/how-to-read-crochet-patterns/) explains the bracketed totals, and [the shape-language Journal note](/blog/how-amigurumi-shapes-grow/) helps you understand what those rounds are doing.

## The four biggest reasons for different results

**1. Yarn changes the scale.** A bulky plush yarn makes larger stitches than a fine cotton, even with exactly the same instructions. Two fibers of similar thickness can drape differently, too. [Choosing yarn for amigurumi](/blog/choosing-yarn-for-amigurumi/) can help you compare options.

**2. Tension changes proportions.** Loose, tall stitches can make a head look longer; short, firm stitches can make it look squatter. You might also crochet more tightly on small pieces than large ones. Test your comfort and consistency in [our tension lesson](/learn/controlling-crochet-tension/).

**3. Stuffing changes the outline.** Uneven filling can make one cheek fuller. Overstuffing may force a carefully shaped body outward and expose gaps. [Our stuffing guide](/blog/the-little-art-of-stuffing-amigurumi/) offers ways to adjust without damaging the fabric.

**4. Assembly changes the expression.** Moving an ear one round upward or angling a nose a little to the side can completely change the face. Pin both sides first and compare them from the front, above, and in profile.

## Try a three-photo diagnosis

Photograph the crochet piece straight on, from the side, and looking down from above. Put the photos next to your reference and ask:

| What looks different? | Check before changing anything |
| --- | --- |
| Overall size | Yarn thickness, hook size and gauge |
| Head looks too long | Stitch height and number of even rounds |
| Body leans | Stuffing distribution and attachment alignment |
| Features look uneven | Centerline and round placement |
| Fabric shows stuffing | Density of stitches and filling amount |

**Don't change stitch counts just to match a photograph.** A count change is a construction change. First check whether the variation is from yarn or placement.

## Pictures have limitations, too

A single photograph is affected by the angle, light, pose, and even how the toy is supported. Illustrations and generated images may not show accurately crochetable stitches or a physically tested sample. Use the pattern's written construction and any clearly documented measurements as the technical reference.

A soft [Cloudberry Lamb](/patterns/cloudberry-lamb-amigurumi/) and a rounded [Woodland Panda](/patterns/woodland-panda-amigurumi/) offer different shapes for practicing these observations. If you feel tempted to keep remaking a piece, make just one small, reversible adjustment at a time.

Your crochet doesn't have to look exactly like a photograph to be lovely. It should, however, match the pattern's critical measurements and construction steps when those matter for fit and safety.
