Making a crochet animal is a small act of patience. One moment you're carefully counting six stitches; the next, there's a mysterious seventh stitch and you're wondering how it got there.

Most amigurumi problems have a simple explanation. The trick is learning to recognize **what changed**, so you can fix the little issue without pulling out an entire afternoon of work.

These are seven of the most useful things to check at the making table.

## 1. Your stitch count keeps changing

**What you see:** a head that grows unevenly, a round that doesn't match the number in brackets, or a piece that suddenly looks lopsided.

**What may be happening:** you missed the first stitch after the marker, worked into the same stitch twice, or skipped a stitch during an increase or decrease.

**Try this:** move a removable marker into the **first actual stitch of each round**, then count before starting the next one. An increase normally adds one stitch to the total; a decrease removes one. If the pattern says a round ends at 24 stitches, treat that number as a friendly checkpoint.

When you're unsure how to read repeats such as \`(sc, inc) × 6\`, our [guide to reading crochet patterns](/learn/how-to-read-crochet-patterns/) can help.

**Little secret:** counting every round while the piece is small is faster than finding a mistake ten rounds later.

## 2. You can see stuffing between the stitches

**What you see:** pale wisps showing through the sides of your animal, especially around the fullest part of a head or body.

**What may be happening:** the fabric is too open for the filling, the piece is overstuffed, or a particularly loose area has stretched.

**Try this:** before beginning a large piece, make a small sample with the yarn and hook you plan to use. For stuffed toys, a hook **smaller than the yarn-label suggestion** often makes a denser fabric, but there is no single perfect size. Choose one that gives firm stitches without straining your hands. If stuffing is already pushing out, gently remove some before deciding whether you need to remake the piece.

Our [yarn-choice guide](/blog/choosing-yarn-for-amigurumi/) explains how fiber, hook size, and fabric density work together.

## 3. Your magic ring has a tiny hole

**What you see:** the center of the first round won't close neatly, or it opens again as you continue.

**What may be happening:** the starting ring wasn't tightened fully, or the tail wasn't secured.

**Try this:** work the opening round as written, then gently pull the starting tail to close the center before crocheting far beyond it. Keep the tail long enough to secure properly inside the finished work. Very fuzzy or slippery yarn can make this harder, so practice first with a smooth strand.

If the first loop still feels mysterious, our [magic-ring lesson](/learn/magic-ring-crochet-in-the-round/) walks through the technique.

## 4. The shape has bumps where you expected smooth curves

**What you see:** ridges at the top of a head, an oddly pointed decrease section, or little corners where the shape should look rounded.

**What may be happening:** increases or decreases are stacked in conspicuous lines, the decreases are worked differently from the pattern, or the filling is pushing the fabric outward.

**Try this:** check the exact decrease method in your pattern. Many amigurumi designs use an **invisible decrease**, but an ordinary decrease is not always interchangeable without changing the look. If the pattern staggers its increases or decreases, keep that placement rather than moving the shaping to the same position every round.

Learn why these choices change a shape in [our shaping notebook](/blog/how-amigurumi-shapes-grow/).

## 5. The first color looks like it's spilling into the next

**What you see:** a stray fleck of the old color at the start of a stripe or a noticeable step where the stripe meets.

**What may be happening:** you changed yarn after finishing the whole stitch, or you're working in a continuous spiral where round boundaries don't line up horizontally.

**Try this:** for a basic single-crochet color transition, introduce the new yarn on the **last draw-through of the previous stitch**. Then follow the specific pattern's instructions for any joined-round or stripe-adjustment method. Not every color jog is a mistake.

We have a dedicated note on [neater amigurumi color changes](/blog/neater-amigurumi-color-changes/).

## 6. The face looks crooked after sewing

**What you see:** a smile that tilts, ears at different heights, or eyes that looked centered until the stuffing went in.

**What may be happening:** parts were attached before the head reached its final shape, or you judged their positions from only one angle.

**Try this:** stuff and shape the head as directed, then use temporary yarn marks to establish a centerline. Position the features and check the face **front-on, from each side, and from above** before securing them. It often takes only one stitch of adjustment to change the expression.

For young children's toys, use securely stitched or embroidered details instead of small detachable plastic eyes or buttons. See our [finishing guide](/blog/little-details-big-personality/) for more.

## 7. The finished toy is unexpectedly floppy or stiff

**What you see:** a head that slumps, limbs that feel hard, or a toy that doesn't have the soft silhouette you expected.

**What may be happening:** the stuffing isn't distributed evenly, seams are too loose, or the yarn and hook produce a fabric different from the intended one.

**Try this:** add stuffing in **small wisps**, pausing to feel for lumps or empty areas. Support the shape without forcing the stitches apart. Check that the joins are secure and placed where the pattern indicates. A firm head and a slightly softer body may feel right for some designs, but the instructions for your particular project should guide you.

### Quick diagnosis: the first thing to check

| What you notice | First place to look |
| --- | --- |
| A round has too many stitches | The first stitch after the marker and repeated increases |
| Stuffing pokes through | Hook-and-yarn density, then stuffing amount |
| A curved part has sharp corners | Decrease method and placement |
| An embroidered face is crooked | The head's centerline and matched rounds |
| A toy tips over | Foot filling, base shape and limb attachment points |

If the main problem is a leaning or slumping character, [Why Your Crochet Animal Won't Sit Straight](/blog/why-crochet-animal-wont-sit-straight/) gives you a more detailed balance check.

### A five-minute check before the final stitch

Before you close the last opening, ask yourself:

- Does my stitch count match the pattern?
- Are the visible stitches dense enough to contain the stuffing?
- Is the filling smooth and reasonably even?
- Are the facial features positioned where I want them?
- Have I secured the tails and joins, and removed every temporary pin?

If something looks odd, you have permission to pause and adjust it. That's part of making, not a reason to feel discouraged.
