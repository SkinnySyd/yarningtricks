Sometimes the sweetest part of a crochet project isn't the main shape. It's the tiny bump on a sheep, the scalloped point at a flower edge, or a little curl that gives a character personality.

Three useful words to know are **bobble**, **picot**, and **loop stitch**. They all make texture, but they do it in completely different ways.

Think of this as a stitch notebook rather than a complete project pattern. We'll practice on spare yarn so you're ready when a special stitch appears in a pattern.

## First, prepare a tiny practice square

Use smooth, medium-weight yarn in a light color and a hook that feels comfortable. Start with a small rectangle of single crochet—perhaps a chain of 13 followed by **12 single crochets** across, then a few more rows of 12.

We use **US crochet terms** throughout this note. In UK terminology, the stitch called *single crochet* here is generally called *double crochet*. Review the [US/UK guide](/learn/how-to-read-crochet-patterns/) if you need a refresher.

Keep a little extra yarn nearby. Special stitches are easier to learn when you don't mind undoing one.

## Bobble stitch: a soft bump

A bobble gathers several partly completed stitches into **one shared top**, giving you a raised spot on the fabric. It can look like a little pebble, a berry, or a playful bit of woolly texture.

There are many bobble variations. Here's **one practice example using four partly completed US double crochets in the same stitch**:

1. Yarn over, insert your hook into the next stitch, yarn over and pull up a loop.
2. Yarn over and pull through **two loops**. Stop before completing the double crochet.
3. Repeat that partial double-crochet movement **three more times in the same stitch**. You now have **five loops on your hook**: the starting loop plus one from each partial double crochet.
4. Yarn over and pull through **all five loops** to close the bobble.

The entire bobble normally counts as **one stitch** in this practice, not four. Work a plain stitch or two afterward, then look at which side the bump wants to sit on. You may want to gently nudge it toward the front.

**A little secret:** if every bobble seems to pop to the *wrong side*, check which side of the work faces you when you make it. Pattern designers sometimes arrange bobble rows from the opposite side so the bumps face outward.

Different patterns may use three, four, or five partial stitches, or a different base stitch entirely. **Always follow that project's special-stitch definition.**

## Picot: a dainty little point

A picot is a tiny chain loop that closes back on itself. It's often used along edges to make a little point, like the tip of a petal or the edge of lace.

For one simple practice picot:

1. Work a regular stitch along your sample's edge.
2. **Chain 3.**
3. Insert your hook into the **first of those three new chains**.
4. Make a **slip stitch** to close the small loop.

You'll see a little point standing above the edge. Some patterns anchor the slip stitch into the previous stitch instead, or use a different number of chains. Neither method is universally correct; the pattern should tell you where the picot closes.

Try spacing your picots apart with two or three ordinary stitches. A few evenly placed points can look tidier than a crowded edge.

## Loop stitch: a playful little fringe

Loop stitch forms an elongated yarn loop on one side of the fabric. Repeating it can make a textured surface like fuzzy hair, soft grass, or a shaggy coat.

Unlike a bobble or picot, **loop stitch has several working methods**. Some wrap the yarn around a finger; some form a measured loop with a hook and then lock it in with the stitch. A pattern's method matters, because an unsecured loop can pull out when the fabric is handled.

To explore the look safely, make a small swatch using the loop-stitch instructions supplied by the project you're following. Keep the loops a consistent length—your finger or a small measuring aid can help—and gently test whether each loop stays secured before continuing.

Looped fabric can snag or tangle, so consider whether the finished item needs frequent washing or might be handled by a very young child. Don't trim loops into fringe unless the pattern and yarn construction specifically support that finish.

## Which texture belongs where?

| Texture | What it looks like | Where it often shines |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bobble** | A rounded raised bump | Fleecy details, tiny berries, textured surfaces |
| **Picot** | A small looped point | Petal tips, delicate borders, decorative edges |
| **Loop stitch** | An elongated strand or loop | Shaggy coats, hair, plush-looking accents |

Mixing techniques is tempting, but a single texture used thoughtfully can be more expressive than three competing details.

## Three habits for tidier texture

**Give each special stitch room.** A bobble can get lost when squeezed between other bulky stitches. If you're experimenting, space the first few apart.

**Read the stitch key.** Abbreviations such as \`bo\`, \`pc\`, and \`lp st\` aren't perfectly universal. A designer's explanation takes priority over a generic tutorial.

**Check the wrong side.** Raised textures have a front and back. Before crocheting a whole section, turn your practice square over and decide which side you want showing.
