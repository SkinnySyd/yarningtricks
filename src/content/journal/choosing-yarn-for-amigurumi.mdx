The yarn shelf is full of possibilities. Cotton in sweet little colors, soft acrylic, fuzzy chenille, and skeins that look like clouds. But the prettiest ball of yarn isn't always the easiest one for your *first* crochet animal.

Amigurumi asks a little more from yarn than a loose scarf does: it needs to hold a shape, make visible stitches, and form a fabric that keeps the stuffing inside.

Here's how to choose without getting lost in the label.

## Begin with the pattern, not the yarn shelf

Look at three things before shopping or choosing from your stash: the **recommended yarn weight**, the **hook size**, and the **expected finished dimensions**.

A pattern designed for a fine, smooth yarn may behave very differently with a chunky fluffy strand. You can sometimes substitute yarn, but the finished toy may change size, proportions, and firmness.

If you're using an existing [Thread & Petal pattern](/patterns/), start with its own supplies and difficulty notes. For a first practice toy, a smooth, light-to-medium-weight yarn usually makes it easier to see where to insert your hook.

## Yarn weight means thickness, not the weight of the ball

A yarn's **weight category** describes roughly how thick the strand is. In the widely used numbered system, **0** is very fine and **7** is very thick.

That's different from the **grams or ounces** printed on a ball. A 50 g ball and a 100 g ball could contain the same yarn thickness but different lengths.

Some labels say **DK**, **worsted**, or **bulky**. Those names are helpful clues, but yarns within one category can still behave differently. Check the label's **yardage/meters**, fiber content, and suggested gauge—not just the category name.

If you'd like a broader reference, the [Craft Yarn Council's yarn-weight guide](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system) explains the categories. Its hook recommendations are *general guidelines*, not fixed rules for stuffed toys.

## Three yarn personalities you'll meet

### Smooth cotton

Cotton often gives defined stitches and a tidy, structured look. It's especially useful when you want to see the shape of each small stitch or keep a crisp edge.

Some cotton yarns have less give than acrylic, so tight amigurumi stitches can feel harder on your hands. If the yarn is splitty or very stiff, a different brand or hook may be more comfortable.

### Everyday acrylic

Acrylic is widely available, comes in many colors, and often has a little stretch. It can feel forgiving when you're practicing a magic ring, increases, or short rounds.

The feel varies widely: one acrylic is smooth and springy, another is fuzzy or slippery. Check the actual strand before assuming they'll behave alike.

### Chenille or plush yarn

Plush yarn makes adorable, cuddly-looking characters. The trade-off is that its fluffy surface can hide the stitch tops, which makes counting and finding the next loop trickier.

Some plush yarns shed or abrade if you repeatedly unravel them. If you're learning a new construction technique, practice it in smooth yarn first. Then return to the plush project with more confidence.

## The hook size is about the fabric you make

A yarn label often suggests a hook for ordinary crochet fabric. **Amigurumi usually needs a closer, denser fabric** so the stuffing doesn't peek through.

For some yarns, using a hook smaller than the label recommendation will help. But don't choose the smallest hook you can find: if your hands hurt or your stitches become impossible to work into, the fabric is *too* tight.

Here's a useful test before starting the whole toy:

1. Crochet a small swatch of single crochet with the yarn and hook you want to use.
2. Stretch it gently with your fingers, as the fabric might stretch over stuffing.
3. Check whether large gaps appear between stitches.
4. If they do, try a slightly smaller hook **or** a different yarn, then compare comfort and density.
5. If the fabric is hard to work, loosen your grip or test a slightly larger hook and reassess the gaps.

There is no universal "best amigurumi hook size." Your personal tension and chosen yarn both matter.

## What if your toy ends up a different size?

Even if you follow every round, changing yarn thickness or tension can change the overall size. A slightly larger stitch gets repeated hundreds of times, so small differences add up.

If your toy needs to fit a particular accessory, opening, or set of pieces, take the pattern's gauge or measurements seriously. For decorative toys, size may be more flexible, but consistent yarn choices across parts still help the pieces fit together.

A useful note to keep with your yarn is: **brand, yarn weight, hook diameter in millimeters, and the feel of the finished fabric**. Future-you will thank you.

## Choose color for visibility and personality

For early practice, a light solid color is easier to read than a dark, busy variegated one. Once you've learned how your stitches look, a contrasting ear, tummy, or beak can give the toy character.

Before buying multiple shades, check whether they are truly compatible in thickness and texture. Two yarns with the same label category can still produce different-sized stitches.

## Care, safety, and the person receiving it

Check the yarn label for washing and drying instructions. If you're gifting a toy, pass those instructions along and consider every material used—stuffing, sewn details, internal supports, and embellishments—not only the outer yarn.

**For babies or young children**, avoid detachable beads, buttons, and plastic eyes; secure embroidered features, seams, and yarn ends carefully. A handmade crochet object isn't automatically suitable for a child just because it feels soft.

If a recipient has known sensitivities, choose materials with their needs in mind and don't assume a yarn is hypoallergenic without evidence.

## A small starting point

Try choosing between one smooth yarn in your stash and a second with a different texture. Make a few rounds of each and ask: which one shows my stitches more clearly, which feels kinder to my hands, and which holds a firm but soft shape?

Then pick a project that makes you smile. The [Cloudberry Lamb](/patterns/cloudberry-lamb-amigurumi/), [Woodland Panda](/patterns/woodland-panda-amigurumi/), and [Milo Woodland Moose](/patterns/milo-woodland-moose-amigurumi/) offer different character styles to explore.

The right yarn isn't always the fanciest one. It's the one that makes *your next stitch* feel possible.
